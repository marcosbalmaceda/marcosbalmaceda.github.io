Profit Expectations and the Depth of Generative AI Use in Small Firms
with J.P. Ronconi (Under review at Research Policy)
Presented: AFE 2026 (U. Chicago); BERD 2026 (Leuven)
Abstract IRB Pre-registration 1 Pre-registration 2 Paper

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) offers small firms an opportunity to increase productivity, yet few use it regularly. We test whether raising owners’ expected returns from AI deepens its use. In a field experiment with 710 micro-business owners in Chile, we randomly assigned demonstrations of a fictional entrepreneur using ChatGPT for her business. The demonstrations raised expectations among owners who expected no gain, by about half a standard deviation. However, we detect no corresponding change in intentions or behavior related to AI use. We document this across measures and horizons: incentivized willingness to pay for AI coaching in the same session, engagement with a free AI course seven weeks later, and self-reported AI use four and a half months after the baseline. Asked what holds back regular use, owners cite money, data privacy, and time.

Platform Certification as a Substitute for Reputation: Evidence from Mandatory AI Disclosure.
with M. Mödl (Revise & Resubmit at Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal)
Presented: G-Forum 2026 (Cottbus), DRUID26 (Copenhagen); AoM 2025 (Copenhagen); BERD 2025 (Namur)
🏆 2026 Winner: G-Forum Norbert Szyperski TIM Research Award · Press release (in German)
Abstract Presentation

We examine how a platform’s certification of a self-reported attribute affects entrepreneurial resource acquisition when reputations differ. In August 2023, Kickstarter required every project to disclose its use of artificial intelligence (AI) and enforced the disclosure, which made AI use observable and credible to backers. In a quasi-natural experiment on 27,845 projects, we find that, relative to comparable non-AI projects, the mandate raised AI-disclosing projects’ probability of reaching the funding goal by 11 percentage points for first-time creators, about whom backers knew little, and lowered it by 13 points for serial creators, whose reputations rest on their own craft. Platform certification thus substitutes for missing reputation, while the same certified fact can contradict an established one, a boundary condition to the complementarity of signals.

Technology Licensing and Inventor Mobility.
with B. Leten, S. Kelchtermans, & E. Melero
Presented: SMS 2026 (Berlin)†; DRUID26 (Copenhagen); EPIP 2025 (Antwerp); Data and Algorithms for ST&I Studies (Leuven)
🏅 2026 Nominee: SMS Annual Conference PhD Paper Prize
🏅 2026 Nominee: SMS Strategic Human Capital Interest Group Best PhD Paper Award
Abstract Presentation

Firms exploit technologies outside their boundaries to earn revenue, yet such decisions also change the employment relationship with the inventors of those technologies. We argue that out-licensing erodes the licensor's informational advantage over these inventors, raising their value to other employers, and that it can lower the value they expect inside the firm. We link the licensing agreements of the largest pharmaceutical firms (2000–2015) to inventor careers. In a difference-in-differences design, inventors whose technology is licensed become ten percentage points more likely to change employer, compared with a baseline rate of 16 percent among their colleagues. The response rises modestly as the value of the licensor's work in the licensed domain declines, and is concentrated among inventors with weaker prior visibility in the labor market.

Defensive Restriction: Process Accountability and the Shadow Use of Artificial Intelligence
with P. Paniagua (Under review at Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization)
Abstract

Many organizations prohibit generative AI that employees keep using on personal devices. We model a three-level game in which a regulator sets how incidents are reviewed, a control unit chooses between managed access and an enforced or unenforced prohibition, and employees decide whether to use consumer tools. When incidents are reviewed against the documented process, the unit answers for use it authorized but not for use it prohibited. Process review therefore produces defensive restriction: the higher their officers’ liability, the more often units prohibit, including where prohibition raises risk. Where review on outcomes is rare and liability moderate, bans stay on paper and can become more attractive the more easily employees evade them. Outcome review with calibrated liability is efficient, and certifying managed programs at moderate cost ends risk-raising prohibitions. A regulator blamed for incidents in permitted activity can prefer process review and refuse certification.

Appropriability Regimes and the Organization of AI Innovation
Sole-authored (Under review at Journal of Economics & Management Strategy)
Abstract Paper

Intellectual property enforcement in artificial intelligence acts on two distinct margins. Courts price the acquisition of training inputs, and they protect proprietary assets from imitation. We model firms that build model quality from appropriable information assets (data, licensed corpora) and from capabilities embodied in researchers, where law can fence only the former and organizational retention only the latter, two distinct exclusion technologies for two distinct inputs. Three results follow. The two enforcement margins tilt the equilibrium source of advantage in opposite directions, and do the same to capability investment and what firms spend to retain talent. The industry’s configuration is itself an equilibrium object, fully open when legal fences are weak, fully closed when they are strong, and mixed on an intermediate band where open firms out-retain their closed rivals. Because a fully open industry’s equilibrium is invariant to output-side enforcement, pooled enforcement-pay estimates mix an exact zero with the positive closed-regime slope and understate the within-closed response. Measured innovation rises with enforcement throughout our benchmark, while welfare peaks in the interior whenever enough of the input bill is real resource cost rather than a transfer to content owners.

Shadow AI and Knowledge Governance in Firms
with P. Paniagua (Under review at Journal of Economics & Management Strategy)
Abstract Paper

Firms restrict employee access to generative AI, and employees conceal the productivity gains they obtain even where access is allowed. We model both responses as equilibria of a single governance game in which the firm chooses an access policy, a costly screen for a contributor tier, and the structure under which extraction of disclosed knowledge is observed and punished. Concealment is a hold-up at the revelation stage. Extraction is observed by the worker but verifiable by no court, so no contract reaches it and the firm’s no-extraction promise must be self-enforcing. Our main result is an ordering of critical discount factors. A patient firm can sustain the promise under bilateral observability, but collective observability sustains it at strictly lower patience, because punishment then scales with the value of the whole knowledge commons rather than with a single relationship. What makes collective observability a design choice rather than an assumption is the shared contribution repository: because every disclosure is on public record, a victim’s report of extraction becomes a claim her peers can check against the record at no cost to her. Where a contributor tier is worth operating, the optimal design is a hybrid of universal managed access, a minimal screen for the tier, and collective observability of extraction. A large-bank AI program illustrates the design.

How Should Mentors Reduce Uncertainty? Theory and Evidence from Job-Seeker Training.
with T. Åstebro, A. Funck, B. Crépon, M. Mensmann, M. Schulte, & N. Pape (Ongoing RCT with France Travail)
Presented: AI Plus Management Consortium 2026 (UCL Management); CFXS 2025 (INSEAD, San Francisco)
Abstract Presentation

We develop a model of mentorship that centers on how mentors reduce uncertainty rather than how they transfer knowledge. Mentors do not merely add information; they filter and reorganize the mentee's decision environment, pruning dominated paths and transmitting heuristics for future evaluation. We formalize two distinct filtering mechanisms and show that they map onto the exploration-exploitation tradeoff at the level of the mentoring interaction. Under internal filtering, the mentor exposes the mentee to varied framings and prompts self-directed reasoning, building the mentee's own capacity to evaluate strategies. Under external filtering, the mentor delivers deterministic, high-confidence guidance, reducing ambiguity directly. We derive conditions under which each mechanism dominates for skill persistence after mentor removal and for mentees differing in initial self-regulatory capacity. We test these predictions in a randomized controlled trial with approximately 90,000 enrolled unemployed job seekers through France Travail, the French national employment agency. Participants in online soft-skills courses — Personal Initiative and Negotiation Skills — are randomly assigned to course content alone, content with an exploratory AI mentor, or content with a directive AI mentor. Both AI mentors share identical self-regulated learning infrastructure — prompting learners through cycles of planning, monitoring, and reflection — and identical course material; they differ only in the filtering mechanism. We cross-randomize across courses to test generalizability. Outcomes span course completion, knowledge retention, entrepreneurial skills, job search, venture creation, employment, income, and wellbeing, measured up to 18 months post-intervention.

Can AI Replace Human Coaches? Evidence from a Field Experiment in Ecuador and Peru.
with I. Asanov, O. Bastani, T. Åstebro, H. Bastani, G. Buenstorf, M. Mensmann, L. Dinarte-Diaz, A. Noha, & A. Hardaker
(RCT launching Q1.2027) [Grant Under Review: Anthropic Economic Futures Research Fund]
Abstract

Individual coaching builds skills that shape the school-to-work transition, but it relies on scarce and costly human time. We run a cluster-randomized trial with about 24,000 final-year secondary students in 800 public schools in Ecuador and Peru to test whether an AI coach can substitute for a human one. Schools are assigned to a placebo course, a digital personal initiative course alone, the course plus six sessions with an AI coach, or the course plus six online sessions with trained human coaches. Both coaches follow the same coaching plan. The AI coach adapts to each student as a human coach would. Baseline data and signals from the sessions, including short checks of understanding, tell it how much help to give, when to encourage, and when to filter options for a student who is stuck between paths. A parallel randomization of teacher training and nudges tests whether teachers' adoption limits delivery. Outcomes include labor market readiness, noncognitive skills, students' uncertainty about which post-school path will work for them, and applications to employers at government youth job fairs. Administrative records then track students into university and work. We also estimate who benefits from each type of coach and test whether assigning students to AI or human coaches by their characteristics beats giving everyone the same type.

El Costo Económico de la Delincuencia: Chile 2013-2022
with L. Hernández & J. Alfaro (Published in Estudios Públicos, 2024)
Presented: Chilean Public Policy Society 2023 (U.Chile)
Abstract PDF DOI

This article examines the economic cost of crime in Chile in the past decade. Following the literature, we categorize the economic losses due to criminal activities into (a) anticipation costs, (b) consequence costs, and (c) response costs, and apply an accounting method to quantify the direct losses due to crime. We consider various types of crime-related costs such as expenses in security, insurance and medical treatments of victims, loss of labor income by victims and inmates, and the economic cost of deaths due to crime, among others. By relying on data from multiple sources such as tax records, government budgets, and official crime reports, we report a lower bound for the economic costs of crime in Chile of 2.1% of GDP in 2022, 46% higher than the cost (in percent of GDP) estimated for 2013 (90% higher if measured in USD of equal purchasing power). We find that in recent years there has been a significant increase in anticipation costs, mainly driven by the rise in security spending by the private sector. Results also show a significant growth in costs due to homicide and vehicle theft cases in recent years — the latter is also a cost borne by the private sector. The paper shows that the cost of crime as a percentage of GDP has increased steadily over the past decade, highlighting the need for a reassessment of security policies focusing on more effective prevention and response strategies. This research contributes to understanding the economic impact of crime in Chile and calls for policies addressing the evolving challenges posed by criminal activities.
